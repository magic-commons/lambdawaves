## Abstract

A picture of electron density cannot tell you which way the electrons are moving. For a molecule the question has a clean answer, because everything λWAVES draws about a molecule comes from one Hermitian matrix, the one-particle density matrix $\Gamma$ in a Gaussian basis: its real part is where the electrons are, its imaginary part is which way they flow. We write the two contractions with their index order and show, on a water orbital packet and with exact controls, that swapping the two indices runs every current backwards and changes no density, so a density image can never catch the mistake; we also explain why phase colour exists only when the matrix has rank one, and even then only up to a common hue rotation the matrix does not record. We say what each of the instrument's models actually supplies. Then we ask whether the currents it draws conserve probability. For any dynamics generated by a projected Hamiltonian, which covers the frozen configuration-interaction-singles (CIS) register, local continuity fails by an explicit source: the part of $H\Psi$ the basis cannot hold acts as a local source and sink, integrating to zero, whose first moment is exactly the gap between the dipole rate and the integrated current. For real-time Hartree–Fock the nonlocal exchange operator, the obvious suspect, is not what breaks continuity; the finite basis is. The frozen-orbital packet is the exception: its generator contains the frozen exchange operator, and on the exported water solution the first moment of its source is 2.3 times the packet's dipole rate, so its net current runs against the motion of its own centre. Finally we describe how a molecular product is stored and drawn and check the description against thirteen native read-backs of one solved H₂O/STO-3G molecule: a float64 evaluation of the same exported data, with truncating half-precision conversion, reproduces every digit read back. FLOW, the instrument's tracer overlay, contracts $\Gamma$ with the correct index order (checked on the exported water data); in the one recorded benzene case its integrated current has the right sense and 0.15 of the right size. No FLOW trajectory, native current or timing result is reported.

## 1. Introduction

A picture of electron density answers where the charge is and is silent about where it is going. For one electron the answer sits in the phase of its wavefunction, because the probability current is $\mathbf j=\rho\nabla\theta$; the companion paper [11] shows this for the hydrogen beat, where two frames of identical density carry opposite currents. A molecule with many electrons has no single three-dimensional wavefunction and so no phase to colour. What it has is the one-particle density matrix, which still determines the density and the current, and which is what λWAVES hands to its renderer and to FLOW, its tracer overlay [1]. The reader can see the object of this paper in the instrument: open MOLECULES on water, choose the MO-REGISTRY's HOMO + LUMO preset, and scrub to a quarter and three quarters of the beat.

This paper asks three questions about that matrix. What are its two contractions, and what does the order of its indices decide (Sections 2 to 4)? Which models of the instrument supply it, and what can colour mean for each (Section 5)? What do gradient evaluation and the continuity equation say about the currents drawn from it (Sections 6 and 7)? Section 8 then describes how a molecular product is stored and drawn and checks the description against read-backs from one solved water molecule, and Section 9 sorts the claims by what supports them. The contributions are of three kinds.

- A convention ledger for the matrix: the creation-first $\Gamma$, the propagator $P$ that the real-time model stores, the sign with which the Fock matrix drives each, and a one-line test that detects an exchanged convention which no density image can show (Sections 2 to 4). This is classical algebra, organised for the instrument. The instrument's own source records that an earlier draft had the indices exchanged.
- A continuity statement for the models that are projected dynamics: for $i\partial_t\Psi=\Pi H\Psi$, $\partial_t\rho+\nabla\cdot\mathbf j$ equals an explicit source built from the omitted part of $H\Psi$, with zero integral. It covers the frozen CIS register directly; a parallel orbital argument shows that the source of real-time Hartree–Fock comes from the finite basis alone; the frozen-orbital packet is the exception, and its exchange-driven source is written out (Section 7). The statement is derived here from standard identities (Ehrenfest's theorem with a residual, the length–velocity inequivalence of incomplete bases, the continuity of complete-basis time-dependent Hartree–Fock). The one numerical record is the app's own, for benzene, and was not re-run.
- A description of how a molecular product is encoded, evaluated and drawn, with consistency checks against native read-backs of water (Section 8). It is an implementation description with a device observation, and it makes no performance claim.

Hartree–Fock theory, density matrices and Gaussian bases [2, 3, 6] are established, as are time-dependent configuration interaction [4] and real-time Hartree–Fock propagation [5]. The loss of local charge conservation in a finite basis is likewise an old acquaintance of the magnetic-response literature, where ring currents are computed and where it drove the continuous-transformation-of-origin methods [17]; laser-driven electronic ring currents of the kind FLOW is meant to show were simulated for Mg-porphyrin two decades ago [18]. What is offered here is the organisation of these facts inside an instrument, the instrument's design and the checks, labelled in Section 9.

## 2. One matrix and two contractions

A Gaussian basis is a non-orthogonal ruler. Atomic-orbital (AO) functions $\chi_\mu$ on different atoms overlap:

$$
S_{\mu\nu}=\int\chi_\mu\chi_\nu\,d^3r\qquad(\chi_\mu\ \text{real}).
\tag{1}
$$

A function $\psi=\sum_\mu c_\mu\chi_\mu$ has norm $c^\dagger Sc$, not $\sum|c_\mu|^2$, and much of the care in this paper is care about that metric. The molecular orbitals (MOs) of the Hartree–Fock calculation are $\phi_k=\sum_\mu C_{\mu k}\chi_\mu$ with $C^{\mathsf T}SC=I$. In that orthonormal basis define the density matrix with the creation index first and the spin summed,

$$
\Gamma_{pq}=\sum_\sigma\langle a^\dagger_{p\sigma}a_{q\sigma}\rangle,\qquad
\Gamma_{\rm AO}=C\,\Gamma\,C^{\mathsf T}.
\tag{2}
$$

$\Gamma$ is what survives of an $N$-electron state when only one-electron questions are asked of it: where is the charge, how fast does it flow, what is its kinetic energy. For a single electron in $\psi=\sum c_\mu\chi_\mu$ it is the outer product of the coefficient vector with itself; for many electrons it is a sum of such pieces and can have any rank up to the basis size. Hermiticity makes $\operatorname{Re}\Gamma$ symmetric and $\operatorname{Im}\Gamma$ antisymmetric. $\Gamma_{\rm AO}$ is a coefficient array for the contractions below; it is not an expectation value of creation operators on the nonorthogonal AOs.

With the spin-resolved field operators $\hat\psi_\sigma(\mathbf r)=\sum_pa_{p\sigma}\phi_p(\mathbf r)$ and real $\phi_p$, the number density is $\sum_\sigma\langle\hat\psi_\sigma^\dagger\hat\psi_\sigma\rangle$ and the number current ($\hbar=m=1$) is $\operatorname{Im}\sum_\sigma\langle\hat\psi_\sigma^\dagger\nabla\hat\psi_\sigma\rangle$. Substituting,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\rho(\mathbf r)&=\sum_{\mu\nu}\operatorname{Re}(\Gamma_{\rm AO})_{\mu\nu}\,\chi_\mu\chi_\nu,\\
\mathbf j(\mathbf r)&=\sum_{\mu\nu}\operatorname{Im}(\Gamma_{\rm AO})_{\mu\nu}\,\chi_\mu\nabla\chi_\nu,\\
N_e&=\operatorname{Tr}(\Gamma_{\rm AO}S).
\end{aligned}
\tag{3}
$$

The split is exact and worth remembering as a slogan: the real part is where, the imaginary part is which way. The density sees only the real part: $\chi_\mu\chi_\nu$ is symmetric in $\mu\nu$, so the antisymmetric imaginary part cancels from it. The current sees only the imaginary part, and because $\operatorname{Im}\Gamma$ is antisymmetric it equals $\tfrac12\sum\operatorname{Im}(\Gamma_{\rm AO})_{\mu\nu}(\chi_\mu\nabla\chi_\nu-\chi_\nu\nabla\chi_\mu)$: an antisymmetric matrix is a list of oriented pairs, and each pair $(\mu,\nu)$ says how much amplitude is circulating from one function into the other. The electron count is the trace against the metric, never a sum over voxels (Section 8). The current counts electrons, so the electrons' charge current is $-\mathbf j$. Equation (3) assumes real spatial functions and zero vector potential; complex gauge-including functions or a vector potential change the current.

For the closed-shell ground state $\Gamma=\operatorname{diag}(2,\dots,2,0,\dots,0)$ in the MO basis, so $\operatorname{Im}\Gamma=0$: it has a density and no current and no phase. For water, $\rho=2\sum_{k\le5}\phi_k^2$ (Figure 3).

## 3. The index order decides the sign of every current

### 3.1. A one-line test

For one electron in $\psi=\sum_\mu c_\mu\chi_\mu$ the matrix is $(\Gamma_{\rm AO})_{\mu\nu}=\bar c_\mu c_\nu$, and Equation (3) gives $\mathbf j=\operatorname{Im}(\bar\psi\nabla\psi)=\rho\nabla\theta$, the hydrogen current of the companion paper. The other index order is the transpose, $\Gamma^{\mathsf T}=\bar\Gamma$, and gives

$$
\rho[\Gamma^{\mathsf T}]=\rho[\Gamma],\qquad
\mathbf j[\Gamma^{\mathsf T}]=-\mathbf j[\Gamma].
\tag{4}
$$

Transposing a Hermitian matrix is complex conjugation, and complex conjugation is time reversal: the transposed matrix is the same movie run backwards, every frame of density unchanged and every current reversed. A density image therefore cannot reveal an exchanged convention, and one comparison with $\operatorname{Im}(\bar\psi\nabla\psi)$ for a complex one-electron amplitude, such as the packet of Section 4.2, can.

### 3.2. Two conventions in one instrument

The real-time model stores the other order, for a good reason. In an orthonormal frame it propagates $\widetilde P=S^{1/2}P_{\rm AO}S^{1/2}$ with $P_{\rm AO}=\sum_kf_kc_kc_k^\dagger$, the occupation-weighted sum over orbital columns, which is the natural object to build from a list of orbitals. For Hermitian matrices $P_{\rm AO}=\Gamma_{\rm AO}^{\mathsf T}=\bar\Gamma_{\rm AO}$: the real parts agree and the imaginary parts have opposite signs, so the adapter that feeds FLOW from the real-time model passes $-\operatorname{Im}P_{\rm AO}$. The two matrices are also driven with opposite sign. Transposing $i\dot{\widetilde P}=[\widetilde F,\widetilde P]$ gives the equation for $\widetilde\Gamma=\widetilde P^{\mathsf T}$:

$$
P_{\rm AO}=\sum_kf_kc_kc_k^\dagger=\Gamma_{\rm AO}^{\mathsf T},\qquad
i\dot{\widetilde P}=[\widetilde F,\widetilde P],\qquad
i\dot{\widetilde\Gamma}=[\widetilde\Gamma,\widetilde F^{\mathsf T}].
\tag{5}
$$

Before a kick $\widetilde F$ is real and the last equation is $i\dot{\widetilde\Gamma}=-[\widetilde F,\widetilde\Gamma]$; once $\operatorname{Im}\widetilde P\neq0$, exchange makes $\widetilde F$ complex, $\widetilde F^{\mathsf T}=\bar{\widetilde F}$ takes its place, and the equation reads $i\dot{\widetilde\Gamma}=-[\bar{\widetilde F},\widetilde\Gamma]$. Index order and the orientation of the commutator travel together: code that mixes the two conventions reverses either the current or the direction of time, and no density test will notice.

The real parts are identical, so the densities are; the imaginary parts have opposite signs, so the currents do. Im$\,\Gamma$ is nonzero only between the out-of-plane O $2p_x$ (the HOMO) and the in-plane $a_1$ functions (the LUMO). The common factor $e^{-i\varepsilon_{\rm H}t}$ cancels from every entry.

### 3.3. Evidence

The instrument's source (`lab/molecular-flow.js`) records that an earlier draft had the two indices exchanged, “which would have drawn every ring current backwards”, and that “no density view could have seen it”; the convention is now fixed and its test compares the contraction with $\operatorname{Im}(\bar\psi\nabla\psi)$ for one complex orbital combination and checks that the charge current $-\mathbf j$ points the way the electronic dipole moves (recorded in the app's test file, not re-run for this paper). For this paper the test was repeated on the solved water data at 40 points with analytic Gaussian gradients, for the HOMO + LUMO packet of Section 4.2: the contraction of Equation (3) equals $\operatorname{Im}(\bar\psi\nabla\psi)$ to $1.7\times10^{-16}$ (the peak current component is $0.40$), the transposed matrix gives the negative to $5.6\times10^{-17}$ with $\rho$ unchanged, and $\mathbf j(T_\rho/4)+\mathbf j(3T_\rho/4)$ vanishes to $1.7\times10^{-16}$ [13]. An archived comparison for benzene [9], made with PySCF values and gradients on 24 points of a ring $0.5$ bohr above the molecular plane, found a largest componentwise discrepancy of $2.2\times10^{-18}$ with the correct orientation and $6.5\times10^{-3}$ with the reversed one, against a peak current component of $3.3\times10^{-3}$. That record comes from a Python recipe and not from the shipped shader.

## 4. A conjugated pair, and what colour can show

### 4.1. The pair

Let $u,v$ be real, orthonormal orbitals and take $\psi_\pm=(u\pm iv)/\sqrt2$. The cross term in the density is purely imaginary and cancels, and in the current it changes sign:

$$
\rho_\pm=\tfrac12(u^2+v^2),\qquad
\mathbf j_\pm=\pm\tfrac12\,(u\nabla v-v\nabla u).
\tag{6}
$$

At the matrix level $\Gamma(\psi_-)=\Gamma(\psi_+)^{\mathsf T}$. Under a real, time-independent Hamiltonian the two states are time reverses of one another, so equal density at one instant does not mean equal density later: if $u,v$ are eigenstates with energies $E_u\neq E_v$ and both states evolve forward from the instant of comparison, $\rho_+-\rho_-=-2uv\sin((E_u-E_v)t)$. Two snapshots of a pendulum at the bottom of its swing look the same; a moment later they do not.

### 4.2. The water packet

The MO-REGISTRY card's HOMO + LUMO preset (amplitude $1/\sqrt2$ and phase $0$ on each of the two lanes) prepares $\psi(\mathbf r,t)=\sum_kc_k\,e^{-i\varepsilon_kt}\phi_k(\mathbf r)$ with $c_{\rm HOMO}=c_{\rm LUMO}=1/\sqrt2$. Its density and current are those of the hydrogen beat with the orbitals of water,

$$
\begin{aligned}
\rho(t)&=\tfrac12\big(\phi_{\rm H}^2+\phi_{\rm L}^2\big)+\phi_{\rm H}\phi_{\rm L}\cos\Delta\varepsilon\,t,\\
\mathbf j(t)&=-\tfrac12\sin(\Delta\varepsilon\,t)\,\big(\phi_{\rm H}\nabla\phi_{\rm L}-\phi_{\rm L}\nabla\phi_{\rm H}\big),
\end{aligned}
\tag{7}
$$

with $u=\phi_{\rm H}=\phi_{1b_1}$ and $v=\phi_{\rm L}=\phi_{4a_1}$. It beats at the frozen-orbital gap $\Delta\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{\rm L}-\varepsilon_{\rm H}=0.99641$ hartree, so $T_\rho=2\pi/\Delta\varepsilon=6.3058$ atomic units ($0.1525$ fs), and not at an excitation energy of the molecule. The distinction matters: an orbital packet is one electron climbing a frozen ladder while the other nine stand still, whereas a real excitation lets all ten rearrange, and the rearrangement roughly halves the energy. The register labels itself “frozen orbitals · beats at $\Delta\varepsilon$, not at $\omega_{\rm RPA}$”, and the first singlet root of the random-phase approximation (RPA) in the instrument's saved PySCF 2.14.0 reference fixture for water is $0.4831$ hartree (read from the fixture, not recomputed here), about half of $\Delta\varepsilon$. Figure 5 compares it with the orbital energies and the RPA roots.

At $t=T_\rho/4$ and $3T_\rho/4$ the relative factor is $-i$ and $+i$, and Equations (6) and (7) apply. Figure 1 shows this natively. The density frames agree: the rendered frames differ in $10{,}307$ of the $8{,}320{,}000$ pixels of the full frame ($0.124\%$, counting a pixel as changed if any colour channel changes), by at most two 8-bit levels, and the read-back maxima of the stored volumes are $0.249353$ and $0.249375$.

The phase frames differ, and they differ by a law. Pointwise, $\arg\psi(3T_\rho/4)=-\arg\psi(T_\rho/4)+141.35^\circ$: the frame at $3T_\rho/4$ is the complex conjugate of the frame at $T_\rho/4$ times $e^{-i\varepsilon_{\rm H}T_\rho}$, a reflection of the hue wheel followed by a turn (over $577{,}796$ pixels the residual of this law has median $-0.5^\circ$; Figure 7a). Written with each frame's own common factor, $\psi(t)=e^{-i\varepsilon_{\rm H}t}(u+e^{-i\Delta\varepsilon t}v)/\sqrt2$, the same map is the conjugated pair $u\mp iv$ with common factors that differ by $e^{-i\varepsilon_{\rm H}T_\rho/2}$, a relative rotation of $70.7^\circ$; how the one map splits into conjugation and rotation is a convention. A picture made of colours therefore cannot isolate the conjugation; the matrices of Figure 4 can.

The two hydrogen levels of the companion paper are commensurate and return exactly at $3T_\rho$ (its Proposition 1); a molecule's orbital energies are not, and its colours never quite come home.

### 4.3. What colour can show

Phase colour is the phase of a one-electron amplitude, and an amplitude exists when $\Gamma$ has rank one. Even then the matrix does not record the common phase that the hue shows: $\Gamma=\bar cc^{\mathsf T}$ is unchanged by $c\to e^{i\gamma}c$. The packet's matrix is rank one; the ground-state matrix of Figure 3 has rank five and the instrument draws it with a flat phase, since the stored amplitude $(\sqrt\rho,0)$ has argument zero. For a many-electron matrix of higher rank there is no single $\theta$, the identity $\mathbf j=\rho\nabla\theta$ no longer holds, and the carrier of phase information is the current itself, which FLOW reads as a velocity $\mathbf v=\mathbf j/\rho$. An orbital packet is also a one-electron picture on the frozen mean-field ladder: it shows what a single-particle excitation of that ladder would look like, not an excited state of the many-electron system; and because it evolves under the frozen, nonlocal Fock operator, its own continuity defect is nonzero even in a complete basis (Section 7).

## 5. Where the matrix comes from

The molecular volume has one owner, and four producers compete for it by priority: the real-time run of the MOLECULES card first, then the STATES register, the ORBITAL register (the two modes of the MO-REGISTRY card) and last the MOLECULES card's own view, which draws the ground-state matrix of Section 2. Each hands over one matrix or one coefficient vector a frame. Table 1 states what the three dynamic producers supply, in the order of this paper's argument, and adds the signed difference, which is formed from two matrices and not supplied by a producer. In the STATES register the state is $\Psi(t)=b_0\Phi_0+\sum_Kb_Ke^{-i\omega_Kt}\Psi_K$, with $\Psi_K$ the singlet states of configuration interaction with singles (CIS, equivalent to the Tamm–Dancoff approximation, TDA) [4] at $\omega_K$ above the RHF determinant $\Phi_0$. Brillouin's theorem makes $\Phi_0$ an eigenvector of the Hamiltonian projected onto the span, so this closed form solves $i\dot\Psi=\Pi H\Psi$ (up to a global phase and to the convergence of the SCF and the CIS eigensolver) at any amplitude and in either time direction; that is the situation of Section 7. With $c_0=b_0/\|b\|$ and $Z(t)=\sum_Kb_Ke^{-i\omega_Kt}X^K/\|b\|$ the blocks of Table 1 follow from the CIS amplitudes $X^K$ (`lab/molecular-register.js` [1]), and the imaginary parts come from interference of the ground state with excited states in $\Gamma_{ov}$ and of excited states with each other in $\Gamma_{oo}$ and $\Gamma_{vv}$. The real-time model keeps the matrix idempotent: a unitary step preserves the eigenvalues of $\widetilde P$ exactly in exact arithmetic and to rounding error when the computed $U$ is unitary (the source records a fixed case in which it was not), so a drifting trace $\operatorname{Tr}(\Gamma_{\rm AO}S)$ points at the overlap transforms and not at the integrator, and a timestep too large to resolve the motion fails silently with a perfectly normalised matrix; convergence of the dipole trace under halving the step has to be checked separately.

A signed difference needs its reference named: the ground state (zero, in the control of Figure 10), the matrix stored when a run was kicked, or the stationary mean of the state, which groups equal-energy pairs with a tolerance of $10^{-8}$. The subtraction is done on matrices, in double precision, before any quantisation (Section 8).

## 6. Gradients and the velocity of FLOW

The current needs derivatives of the basis, and Gaussians give them in closed form. For a contracted Cartesian Gaussian $\chi=x_A^ay_A^bz_A^c\sum_pd_pe^{-\alpha_pr_A^2}$ centred at $\mathbf A$, with $s_0=\sum_pd_pe^{-\alpha_pr_A^2}$ and $s_1=\sum_p\alpha_pd_pe^{-\alpha_pr_A^2}$,

$$
\partial_x\chi=\big(a\,x_A^{a-1}s_0-2\,x_A^{a+1}s_1\big)\,y_A^bz_A^c,
\tag{8}
$$

and the other components follow by exchanging coordinates (the first term vanishes for $a=0$). FLOW contracts these analytic expressions with $\operatorname{Im}\Gamma_{\rm AO}$ at each tracer position. It does not differentiate the stored volume, for the reason given in §6.1 of the companion paper [11]: a small value error can coexist with a large derivative error, so the display texture cannot supply the current.

FLOW advances a tracer by $\mathbf v=\mathbf j/\rho$ where $\rho$ exceeds a floor of $10^{-7}$ atomic units, with a speed cap and midpoint (second-order Runge–Kutta) steps; the matrices are rebuilt once per frame and the tracers ride the frozen field between rebuilds. Dividing by the density is the delicate step: where the cloud is thin a tiny error in $\mathbf j$ or $\rho$ becomes a large error in the speed. If $|\tilde\rho-\rho|\le\delta_\rho<\rho$ and $\|\tilde{\mathbf j}-\mathbf j\|\le\delta_j$, then $\tilde{\mathbf v}-\mathbf v=[(\tilde{\mathbf j}-\mathbf j)\rho-\mathbf j(\tilde\rho-\rho)]/(\rho\tilde\rho)$ and $\tilde\rho\ge\rho-\delta_\rho$ give

$$
\|\tilde{\mathbf v}-\mathbf v\|\le\frac{\delta_j+\|\mathbf v\|\,\delta_\rho}{\rho-\delta_\rho}.
\tag{9}
$$

The bound blows up where $\rho$ approaches its own uncertainty: a modest current error then makes a large velocity error, and the fixed floor is a numerical guard, not a calibrated uncertainty. The cap changes the drawn speed, and smaller tracer steps cannot remove either the finite-basis error of Section 7 or the error of holding the field for a frame. Tracers are a reading of the current and not particles. No FLOW trajectory is part of this paper.

## 7. Continuity in a projected model

### 7.1. The scalar identity and its source

Probability can only move by flowing: that is the continuity equation, and it holds for any solution of the Schrödinger equation with a local potential. A finite basis breaks it in a specific way. When the Hamiltonian acts on a state, part of the result lies outside the basis and is thrown away; the thrown-away part behaves at each point like a local source or sink of probability, and because the norm is still conserved these sources sum to zero over space. Probability does not leak out of the molecule; it is teleported within it. The rest of this section makes that exact.

For one electron with a local real Hamiltonian $H=-\tfrac12\nabla^2+V$, define the residual $R=i\partial_t\psi-H\psi$. Differentiating $|\psi|^2$ and using $\nabla\cdot\operatorname{Im}(\bar\psi\nabla\psi)=\operatorname{Im}(\bar\psi\nabla^2\psi)$,

$$
\partial_t|\psi|^2+\nabla\cdot\operatorname{Im}(\bar\psi\nabla\psi)=2\operatorname{Im}(\bar\psi R).
\tag{10}
$$

If $R=0$ this is the continuity equation. An orthogonal projector $\Pi$ onto a finite basis, with $i\partial_t\psi=\Pi H\psi$ and $\psi=\Pi\psi$, gives $R=-(1-\Pi)H\psi$: the omitted part of $H\psi$ is a local source. Its integral is zero, since $\int\bar\psi R=-\langle\psi|(1-\Pi)H\psi\rangle=0$, so the norm is conserved while the local equation fails. A hydrogen illustration is exact and small enough to see whole: for the two-level model $(1s,2p_z)$ in a field $F$ one has $R=-F(1-\Pi)z\psi$, and for $\psi=a\phi_{1s}+b\phi_{2p_z}$ the source is $2Fz_{12}\operatorname{Im}(\bar ab)\,(\phi_{1s}^2-\phi_{2p_z}^2)$ with $z_{12}=\langle1s|z|2p_z\rangle=128\sqrt2/243$. It vanishes only on the surface $\lvert\phi_{1s}\rvert=\lvert\phi_{2p_z}\rvert$, reaches $2.3\times10^{-2}$ at the nucleus (for $a=0.6$, $b=0.8i$ and $F=0.1$), and has zero integral because both orbitals are normalised. On the $+z$ axis it changes sign at $2.04$ bohr. Figure 9 maps it: probability appears near the nucleus and disappears in the outer shell, with no current between.

### 7.2. Many electrons, and the instrument's models

The many-electron statement has the same shape, and the same proof: Ehrenfest's theorem with a residual term. It is numbered after Proposition 1 of the companion paper [11].

> **Proposition 2.** Let $\Psi(t)$ be an $N$-electron state in the range of a time-independent orthogonal projector $\Pi$ with $i\partial_t\Psi=\Pi H\Psi$, where $H$ has real local potentials (possibly time dependent) and no vector potential, and let $R=-(1-\Pi)H\Psi$, $\hat\rho(\mathbf r)=\sum_i\delta(\mathbf r-\mathbf r_i)$, $\hat{\mathbf r}=\sum_i\mathbf r_i$ and $\mathbf j(\mathbf r)=\operatorname{Im}\sum_i\langle\Psi|\delta(\mathbf r-\mathbf r_i)\nabla_i\Psi\rangle$, the number current of Equation (3). Then $\partial_t\rho+\nabla\cdot\mathbf j=2\operatorname{Im}\langle\Psi|\hat\rho(\mathbf r)R\rangle$, the source integrates to zero, and $\tfrac{d}{dt}\langle\hat{\mathbf r}\rangle=\int\mathbf j\,d^3r+2\operatorname{Im}\langle\Psi|\hat{\mathbf r}R\rangle$.

*Proof.* Write $i\partial_t\Psi=H\Psi+R$. For any Hermitian $A$, $\tfrac{d}{dt}\langle A\rangle=i\langle[H,A]\rangle+2\operatorname{Im}\langle\Psi|AR\rangle$. For $A=\hat\rho(\mathbf r)$ and local potentials, $i\langle[H,\hat\rho]\rangle=-\nabla\cdot\mathbf j$, the usual identity. Integrating over space replaces $\hat\rho$ by the number operator, so the source integrates to $2N\operatorname{Im}\langle\Psi|R\rangle=-2N\operatorname{Im}\langle\Psi|(1-\Pi)H\Psi\rangle=0$ because $\Psi=\Pi\Psi$. For $A=\hat{\mathbf r}$, $i\langle[H,\hat{\mathbf r}]\rangle=\langle\sum_i\mathbf p_i\rangle=\int\mathbf j\,d^3r$. $\square$

The third statement is the one with teeth. The centre of the electron cloud can move in two ways on paper: as the time derivative of the mean position (the length form), or as the integrated current (the velocity form). For an exact solution they are the same number. In a projected model they differ by exactly the first moment of the source, so the gap between them is a direct, global measurement of how badly local continuity fails, and it needs no grid.

The frozen CIS register is this situation, with $\Pi$ the projector onto the reference and the singles in the finite basis; since $H$ couples the singles to doubles, its defect has two sources, the configuration truncation and the finite basis. The operator algebra of the proof ($\tfrac{d}{dt}\langle A\rangle=i\langle[H,A]\rangle+2\operatorname{Im}\langle\Psi|AR\rangle$ and $\langle\Psi|R\rangle=0$) holds to rounding error on 300 random finite instances, and the full statement, including the lattice form of $i\langle[H,\hat\rho]\rangle=-\nabla\cdot\mathbf j$ and the dipole relation, on a two-particle lattice with local potentials, a field and a random projector (50 trials, residuals below $2\times10^{-15}$). Two consequences matter for the instrument. Refining the display grid cannot cure the defect, since it changes the sampling of $\Psi$ and not $\Pi$; only a larger basis or configuration space can shrink it. And the gap between the dipole rate and the integrated current is the first moment of the source: the time derivative of the length form $\langle\hat{\mathbf r}\rangle$ and the velocity form $\langle\sum_i\mathbf p_i\rangle$ differ by exactly $2\operatorname{Im}\langle\Psi|\hat{\mathbf r}R\rangle$.

Hartree–Fock needs one more step, because its exchange operator is nonlocal, and a nonlocal operator is exactly the kind of thing one expects to break a local conservation law. It does not, and the reason is a small miracle worth seeing. For closed-shell time-dependent Hartree–Fock in a complete basis the orbitals obey $i\partial_t\phi_k=(-\tfrac12\nabla^2+V+J-K)\phi_k$, where $J$ is the local, real Hartree potential of $\rho=2\gamma(\mathbf r,\mathbf r)$ and $K$ is the exchange operator with kernel $\gamma(\mathbf r',\mathbf r)/|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|$. The exchange term enters the density equation through

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_k\bar\phi_k(\mathbf r)\,(K\phi_k)(\mathbf r)&=\int\frac{|\gamma(\mathbf r,\mathbf r')|^2}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|}\,d^3r',\\
\gamma(\mathbf r,\mathbf r')&=\sum_k\bar\phi_k(\mathbf r)\phi_k(\mathbf r'),
\end{aligned}
\tag{11}
$$

which is real (had the kernel been $\gamma(\mathbf r,\mathbf r')/\lvert\mathbf r-\mathbf r'\rvert$, the sum would be $\int\gamma(\mathbf r,\mathbf r')^2/\lvert\mathbf r-\mathbf r'\rvert\,d^3r'$, which is not real in general), so it contributes nothing to $\operatorname{Im}\sum_k\bar\phi_kF\phi_k$ and the complete-basis equations obey local continuity. Each orbital, taken alone, does leak through exchange; summed over the occupied set the leaks cancel point by point, because the exchange of orbital $k$ with orbital $l$ is the conjugate of the exchange of $l$ with $k$. In a finite basis the orbitals obey $i\partial_t\phi_k=\Pi F\phi_k$, and with $\rho=2\sum_k|\phi_k|^2$ the same steps give $\partial_t\rho+\nabla\cdot\mathbf j=4\operatorname{Im}\sum_k\bar\phi_kR_k$ with $R_k=-(1-\Pi)F\phi_k$ and zero integral. For real-time Hartree–Fock the nonlocality of exchange is not what breaks continuity; the finite basis is. (Equation (11) holds to rounding error for random complex orbitals and a random real symmetric kernel, 200 trials.)

The ORBITAL register is the exception, and now it is clear why. Its packet $\psi=\sum_kc_ke^{-i\varepsilon_kt}\phi_k$ is one electron evolving under the frozen operator $F_0=-\tfrac12\nabla^2+V+J_0-K_0$ of the ground state, not the set of all occupied orbitals, so the cancellation of Equation (11) does not apply: the packet feels the exchange of the other nine electrons as an external nonlocal field, and nobody cancels its leak. Even with exact eigenfunctions, $F_0\phi_k=\varepsilon_k\phi_k$ in a complete basis, the residual against $-\tfrac12\nabla^2+V$ is $R=(J_0-K_0)\psi$, the real local $J_0$ drops out of $\operatorname{Im}(\bar\psi R)$, and for the packet of Equation (7)

$$
\partial_t\rho+\nabla\cdot\mathbf j=-2\operatorname{Im}(\bar\psi K_0\psi)
=\sin(\Delta\varepsilon\,t)\,\big(\phi_{\rm H}K_0\phi_{\rm L}-\phi_{\rm L}K_0\phi_{\rm H}\big),
\tag{12}
$$

which is generally nonzero at a point and has zero integral because $K_0$ is symmetric. For this model exchange is the cause of the defect, and the only one in a complete basis; the packet of Figures 1 and 4 therefore carries a current whose local continuity the instrument does not claim. (Equation (12) holds to rounding error on a lattice with a random symmetric nonlocal $K_0$.)

How big is the effect on the water packet? The dipole gap of Proposition 2 answers without a grid. For the packet of Equation (7) only the $x$ component of the transition moments is nonzero: $x_{\rm HL}=\langle\phi_{\rm H}|x|\phi_{\rm L}\rangle=0.07404$ bohr and $g_{\rm HL}=\langle\phi_{\rm H}|\partial_x|\phi_{\rm L}\rangle=-0.09772$ bohr$^{-1}$ (analytic one-electron integrals, PySCF 2.14.0, exported coordinates and orbitals; midpoint quadrature agrees to $4\times10^{-4}$ and gives $0.99999$ for the hydrogen pair of the companion paper). The dipole rate is $d\langle x\rangle/dt=-\Delta\varepsilon\,x_{\rm HL}\sin\Delta\varepsilon t$ and the integrated current is $\int j_x\,d^3r=-g_{\rm HL}\sin\Delta\varepsilon t$, so $q=g_{\rm HL}/(\Delta\varepsilon\,x_{\rm HL})$, which equals $1$ for eigenfunctions of a local Hamiltonian (Equation 8 of the companion paper). Here $q=-1.32$ (Figure 6c): the integrated current has the opposite sign to the dipole rate and $1.32$ times its size. In words, while the packet's centre of charge moves to the right, its net current flows to the left. Integrating Equation (10) against $\mathbf r$ gives $\int\mathbf r\,(\partial_t\rho+\nabla\cdot\mathbf j)\,d^3r=(1-q)\,d\langle\mathbf r\rangle/dt$, so the first moment of the source is $2.32$ times the dipole rate; it collects the frozen exchange operator of Equation (12) and the finite basis. As a check on the length form, $\sqrt2\,x_{\rm HL}=0.1047$ against $0.1006$ for the first RPA transition dipole in the saved reference (`verification/packet_current_checks.py`, `packet_current_pyscf_check.py`).

### 7.3. A recorded measurement

The app's report and test file record one measurement, for benzene in STO-3G with the STATES register's ring preparation (the ground state plus two degenerate singlet CIS states a quarter turn apart in phase) (`REPORT.md` and `tests/molecular-flow.test.mjs` [1]). On 400 random points of a box around the molecule the relative defect $\|\partial_t\rho+\nabla\cdot\mathbf j\|/\|\partial_t\rho\|$, taken with finite differences at $t=0$, is $1.19$. At $t=3$ atomic units the in-plane integrated charge current $-\int\mathbf j\,d^3r$ is parallel to the rate of change of the electronic dipole (cosine above $0.999$) but has $0.15$ of its magnitude. With $\langle\hat{\mathbf r}\rangle=\langle\sum_i\mathbf r_i\rangle$ ($N$ times the electrons' mean position; the electronic dipole is $-\langle\hat{\mathbf r}\rangle$) this says $\int\mathbf j=0.15\,d\langle\hat{\mathbf r}\rangle/dt$ in the plane, so in the terms of Proposition 2 the first moment of the source, $2\operatorname{Im}\langle\Psi|\hat{\mathbf r}R\rangle$, is parallel to $d\langle\hat{\mathbf r}\rangle/dt$ and carries about $85\%$ of it: in this minimal-basis case the length and velocity forms of the dipole rate disagree by that much. The two numbers belong to different instants and the integral used a coarse grid, so the reading is indicative. The proposition names the term and does not measure it, and the record was not re-run here. The app concludes that the sense of FLOW's current is right in this case and its magnitude qualitative; Proposition 2 shows why neither follows in general.

## 8. Storing and drawing a molecular product

### 8.1. The kernel

One compute kernel fills the volume, one invocation per voxel centre in $4\times4\times4$ workgroups. Each invocation evaluates every AO at its point into a table in workgroup memory, laid out so that neighbouring invocations read adjacent addresses, and then contracts: $\psi=\sum_\mu c_\mu\chi_\mu$ for an orbital, and for a density the symmetric half-sum $\rho=\sum_\mu D_{\mu\mu}\chi_\mu^2+2\sum_{\mu<\nu}D_{\mu\nu}\chi_\mu\chi_\nu$ with $D=\operatorname{Re}\Gamma_{\rm AO}$, which is the real part of Equation (3) with each off-diagonal pair counted once. Functions on one centre with one exponent list share their $e^{-\alpha r^2}$ evaluations: STO-3G water needs 12 exponentials a point instead of 15, benzene 54 instead of 72 (exact counts from the saved basis table; no speed is claimed). Three pipelines hold 16, 40 or 64 AOs, with single-precision tables of 4, 10 and 16 KiB, and the smallest that fits is used (water 16, benzene 40); the dead table is reused for the workgroup maximum. The limit is 64 AOs, and lowering the rank of $\Gamma$ does not lift it, since basis evaluation and the table still scale with the number of AOs.

### 8.2. Four products, one texture

The cache is `rgba16float`, and the meaning of its two main channels depends on the product (Figure 10). A density is stored as $(\sqrt\rho,0)$, a numerical encoding that carries no phase and no current. A real orbital is stored as $(\phi,0)$ and a complex orbital as $(\operatorname{Re}\psi,\operatorname{Im}\psi)$. A signed matrix is stored as $(\sum\Delta D\,\chi\chi,0)$ with its sign and no square root; squaring would erase gain against loss. The kernel's maximum statistic is $\max(R^2+G^2)$ for every kind, and the signed view divides by its square root, so $\pm1$ is the extreme lobe.

## 9. Scope and evidence

Table 3 sorts the claims of this paper by what supports them. The captures show a single ground-state solution of a fixed-geometry, minimal-basis water molecule and the instrument drawing what its equations say about it; they do not show motion. The identities of Sections 3, 4 and 7 are exact for the stated model (checked numerically on the solved data and on random and lattice instances [13]), and the instrument's pictures of a many-electron current rest on a minimal basis whose continuity defect, on the one record available, is of order one. Everything beyond that, and the question of what is new in the instrument's design, is for the instrument's authors and for experiments not done here. The companion paper [11] treats the hydrogen beat that Figure 1 mirrors.

Each *not established* item is likewise a measurement: a kicked real-time run with a recorded FLOW trajectory and native current, native error fields against PySCF, a non-zero signed difference, and timings on more than one device.

## 10. Summary

Every molecular density and current in λWAVES is a contraction of one Hermitian matrix: the real part is where, the imaginary part is which way, and the order of the indices, which no density image shows, is the arrow of time and fixes the sign of every current (Sections 2 to 4). Phase views need a rank-one $\Gamma$ and fix the hue only up to a common rotation (Section 4.3). In a projected model probability is conserved globally but not locally: the part of $H\Psi$ the basis cannot hold acts as a source with zero integral, and the gap between the dipole rate and the integrated current measures it without a grid; for real-time Hartree–Fock the finite basis, not exchange, is the cause, while the frozen-orbital packet has an exchange-driven source of its own, whose first moment on the exported water solution is 2.3 times its dipole rate, so its net current runs against its own centre (Section 7). Thirteen native read-backs of one solved water molecule are reproduced by float64 evaluation with truncating half-precision conversion (Section 8).
